It was early October, and the industry's projections pointed to saving the month. Hoteliers expected [to reach 60% between 2 and 12 October and climb above 82%](https://www.bluradio.com/regiones/caribe/sector-turistico-de-santa-marta-pide-politicas-de-estado-para-garantizar-seguridad-pr30) over the closing holiday weekend. Reality was different: the tourism sector recorded an overall average occupancy that did not exceed 33%, against 49% measured at the same time the previous year.

When I watch a season's numbers fall, the usual response always looks the same to me: more promotion, with campaigns and discounts to encourage travelers to come. The institutional premise assumes that empty rooms are a marketing failure. But when you have to make payroll at the end of the month, it becomes clear that the problem is not solved by selling cheap a service whose basic cost structure is broken.

## Why does cutting rates to revive tourism so often speed up bankruptcy?

Cutting prices during a hotel crisis in Santa Marta looks like a logical way to fill beds, but on the Caribbean coast it often turns into a liquidity trap. Every extra guest uses kilowatts of air conditioning and liters of drinking water. If the nightly rate drops suddenly to compete with mass promotions, the gross margin disappears.

During critical public-order episodes, such as security alerts on the roads, the collapse in bookings does not respond to price. Coverage documented that district hotel occupancy [fell below 38%](https://www.infobae.com/colombia/2026/09/24/paro-armado-de-los-pachenca-genera-preocupacion-al-gremio-turistico-de-santa-marta-hoteles-reportan-caidas-de-reservas-para-la-semana-de-receso-de-octubre/), while in the rural corridor toward Tayrona National Park it plunged to 15% during the armed strike by Los Pachenca. In a context of paralysis or fear, nobody decides to travel simply because a room costs a few percent less. Cutting the rate at those moments only weakens the business's cash against the few visitors who do decide to come.

## How much does it really cost to keep a room open when the energy bill doubles?

Hotel profitability in Santa Marta is not lost at marketing desks; it drains away in machine rooms and utility bills. Electricity is a disproportionate burden that no institutional discount offsets. In the city, small hotels [went from paying roughly COP 2 million to a little over COP 4 million a month for energy](https://www.eltiempo.com/colombia/otras-ciudades/altos-costos-de-energia-tienen-padeciendo-a-comerciantes-en-santa-marta-700239). In areas such as El Rodadero, simple properties had to absorb bills of COP 10 to 12 million, while in Pozos Colorados bills went from COP 12 to 14 million to a range of COP 17 to 20 million.

I looked for the consolidated percentage increase in commercial electricity rates charged by Air-e in Magdalena over the last 12 months, but I did not find a unified official figure. What the cited coverage does say is that small hotels' energy bills doubled, and that overall occupancy did not exceed 33%. The two together are what drain the cash.

Water is different: there is no data. I looked for a reliable public record of the average cost per cubic meter, or per tanker-truck trip, of water in hotel areas of Santa Marta and El Rodadero compared with standard utility rates, and there is no verifiable figure in official sources. Without that figure I cannot tell you how much water weighs on a small hotel's cash; what is documented is the electricity bill.

## What do the sector's numbers show, and what can't be measured yet?

Aggregate hotel averages tend to hide how fragmented the market is. I looked for the official figures on hotel occupancy and the drop in commerce sales reported by Cotelco Magdalena and Fenalco for the last quarter in Santa Marta, but I found no consolidated, verifiable quarterly reports in the sources I consulted. What the spot measurements do show is how uneven the blow was: district occupancy below 38%, 15% in the corridor toward Tayrona National Park, and an overall average that did not exceed 33%, against 49% the year before.

Informal lodging is another factor people mention. I looked for the formal trade-association estimate of the volume of informal tourist beds versus registered hotel beds in the district, and I found no verifiable public figure either. Without that figure I won't claim how much it moves prices. What I can say is that the small formal operator has to cover payroll, taxes and an electricity bill that doubled, and that leaves little room to compete on volume with low rates.

## Which operating adjustments do protect margin during a prolonged low season?

When occupancy does not exceed 33%, running the property as if the house were full burns through capital reserves in a few weeks. If I were running a small lodging in this situation, my priority would not be to invent discounts or print flyers with institutional slogans, but to stop avoidable consumption:

1. **Isolating room inventory.** Keep whole modules or floors closed so you are not paying for passive cooling or maintenance in empty spaces.
2. **Strict review of cooling equipment.** Adjust set points in common areas and restrict running motors and pumps at times when the rate or the demand penalizes consumption.
3. **Preserving the base price.** Accept that demand held back by outside factors does not respond to price incentives. Selling a few rooms at a rate that covers fixed costs leaves more net cash than filling the hotel at subsidized rates that drive up utility consumption.

If occupancy does not respond to price cuts, the only immediate lever the business has left is the switch: how many rooms can you disconnect completely today so the utility bill does not eat the margin you have left?
