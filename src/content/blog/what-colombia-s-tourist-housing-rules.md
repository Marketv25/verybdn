## What exactly do I need to register and with which entity?

You register with the Registro Nacional de Turismo (RNT), administered by the Ministerio de Comercio, Industria y Turismo. The registry exists for any property offering paid accommodation to tourists, whether you list it on Airbnb, Booking, your own website or take bookings by WhatsApp.

The RNT is not optional if you charge money and guests stay fewer than 30 consecutive days. The ministry defines "vivienda turística" as any residential property used for tourist lodging. If you live there and rent a room, you register. If you own a flat in Cartagena and rent it by the week, you register. The line is drawn at commercial use, not at scale.

Registration requires:

- RUT (tax registration) showing the correct economic activity code for tourist accommodation
- Proof of legal use of the property: ownership deed, lease with owner authorisation, or usufruct agreement
- Certificate from the local fire department or civil defence verifying basic safety measures
- Liability insurance policy covering guests
- Completed forms, payment of registration fee

The municipality may also require a land use certificate confirming the property can legally be used for short-term rentals. Bogotá, Medellín and Cartagena enforce this more strictly than smaller towns. In Minca, no one has asked me for one yet. In Parque 93, they will.

## How much does compliance actually cost, including the hidden expenses?

The RNT registration fee itself is not the problem. Processing the supporting documents is.

You will pay:

- RNT registration fee: varies by property type and capacity, typically between COP $200,000 and COP $600,000 for a small property. This is a one-time fee, though renewal filings are required.
- Insurance: COP $300,000 to COP $800,000 annually depending on capacity and coverage. The law does not specify a minimum amount, but most operators carry at least COP 50 million in liability coverage because that is what insurers offer as the entry product.
- Fire safety inspection and certificate: COP $150,000 to COP $400,000. Repeat every two years in most municipalities.
- Accountant or gestor to prepare documents if you do not do it yourself: COP $500,000 to COP $1,500,000. This is where many small operators stall. The forms are not intuitive and mistakes delay processing by months.
- Land use certificate from the planning office (if required): COP $50,000 to COP $200,000.

Total first-year cost to comply: COP $1,200,000 to COP $3,500,000, depending on where you are and whether you hire help.

That is not counting the time cost. If you do it yourself, expect to visit four or five different offices, submit documents, wait, resubmit corrections, wait again. I have seen applications take three months in Bogotá and eight months in smaller cities where the tourism office has two employees.

The hidden cost is this: while you wait for approval, you cannot legally operate. But you also cannot afford to stop operating, so you continue informally and hope the process finishes before someone notices.

## What happens if I'm operating without registration right now?

You are technically liable for fines, but enforcement is inconsistent.

The regulatory framework allows fines starting at around COP $2,000,000 and escalating based on the severity and duration of non-compliance. In practice, most operators never see an inspector unless a guest files a formal complaint with the Superintendencia de Industria y Comercio or the local tourism office, or a neighbour reports you to the municipality.

Platforms like Airbnb now require hosts to provide an RNT number to keep listings active in certain cities. Bogotá, Cartagena and Medellín have enforcement agreements with the ministry. If you list there without an RNT, the platform may suspend your account after a warning period. In smaller towns, this has not happened yet.

The larger risk is not the fine. A guest gets hurt and sues, your insurance does not cover you because you were operating illegally. A guest refuses to pay, you cannot escalate it through formal channels. A neighbour complains about noise, the municipality can shut you down immediately because you have no permit to argue from.

Registration does not protect you from all problems, but it gives you a position to argue from. Without it, you are entirely at the mercy of whoever decides to make an issue of it.

## Does registration actually protect me from guest disputes or just expose me to more oversight?

Both.

The RNT makes you visible to regulators, which means you are subject to inspections and complaints. If a guest reports you to the Superintendencia, the ministry will investigate. If you are registered, they check whether you met your obligations. If you are not registered, the investigation starts with the fact that you are operating illegally, and everything else is secondary.

But registration also gives you legal standing. If a guest damages your property, you can pursue it through civil courts as a registered business. If a guest files a fraudulent chargeback, you have documentation showing you operate a legitimate, regulated lodging business. Platforms and payment processors take you more seriously.

Insurance only covers you if you are compliant. Every policy I have seen includes a clause voiding coverage if you were operating without required permits at the time of the incident. That means if a guest is injured in your unregistered property, you pay out of pocket. If your registered neighbour faces the same situation, their insurer handles it.

The oversight is real. Once you are registered, the ministry can request documentation, inspect your property, verify you are meeting standards. For operators who have been running informally for years, this feels like an intrusion. For guests, it is the only assurance that someone is checking.

## Are the rules different if I live on the property versus renting it out entirely?

No. The registration requirement is the same.

The distinction the law makes is between tourist accommodation and long-term residential rental. If you rent a room in your house to a tourist for a week, you register. If you rent the same room to a student for six months, you do not.

Some municipalities have additional restrictions on short-term rentals in residential buildings. Bogotá allows it only in properties where the owner lives on-site or in buildings where the homeowners' association has explicitly approved it. Medellín restricts it in certain residential zones. Cartagena allows it almost everywhere but requires the land use certificate.

Living on the property does not exempt you from registration, but it can simplify some of the paperwork. Fire safety inspections are easier when you are present to coordinate them. Insurance is sometimes cheaper because the property is occupied full-time. And practically, neighbours complain less if they see you are living there and managing guest behaviour.

But from the ministry's perspective, you are operating a commercial lodging business, and the rules apply in full.

## What is the actual risk calculation here?

You are weighing the cost and time of compliance against the probability of enforcement and the consequence if caught.

If you operate in a building where neighbours already complain, in a city with active enforcement, on a platform that now requires RNT numbers, the risk of continuing informally is high. You will either be reported or delisted, and at that point compliance becomes more expensive because you are doing it under pressure.

If you operate in a small town, in a standalone property, with no neighbour complaints, the immediate risk is lower. But you are still uninsured, still operating without legal standing, still one guest incident away from a problem you cannot manage.

Many small operators stay informal not because they calculated the risk and accepted it, but because they do not know how to start the process, cannot afford to stop operating while they wait for approval, and do not trust that compliance will actually protect them from anything.

That is the gap the regulation has not solved. It created a standard, but not a path for people already operating to reach it without losing income they cannot afford to lose.

The honest answer: if you are starting now, comply from the beginning. The cost is manageable if you plan for it. If you are already operating informally, start the process and accept that you will be in a grey zone for a few months while it completes. The risk of doing nothing is not that you will definitely be fined. You will have no position to argue from when something goes wrong.
