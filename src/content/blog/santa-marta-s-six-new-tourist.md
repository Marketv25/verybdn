## Where are the six piers actually going?

The announcement exists. The locations do not, at least not in any document an operator can use to plan around. Six piers sounds like distribution: Rodadero, El Morro, maybe Taganga, possibly Pozos Colorados. But without published coordinates or even neighbourhood confirmations, you are guessing whether the infrastructure lands near your operation or an hour away.

This matters because a pier is not neutral. It becomes the departure point. If your guests currently walk from your property to the beach where your boat waits, and a pier goes up two bays over, you now route them through someone else's infrastructure. Or you move your operation. Or you lose the booking to whoever sits at the pier.

Santa Marta has one functioning tourist pier at the marina. It is not a model of open access.

## Will operators working now get berthing rights at the new piers?

No one has said. The optimistic read is that these piers expand capacity for the boats already working: the pangas running to Playa Cristal, the day tour operators doing snorkel circuits, the fishing-and-lunch outfits. Build the infrastructure, formalise the routes, reduce the insurance exposure of beach launches in swell.

The pessimistic read is that six piers means six concessions, and concessions go to operators with capital or connections, not to the guy who has been running a Boston Whaler out of Taganga for eight seasons.

Pier berthing is not first-come. It is contracted or it is auctioned or it is allocated by a harbour authority. If the allocation process has been designed, it has not been published. If it has not been designed, it will be designed under pressure once the concrete is poured, which is when the decisions get made fast and in favour of whoever is already in the room.

## What does a tourist pier regulate that a beach departure does not?

A pier formalises the transaction. You are no longer negotiating with a boat owner on the sand. You are buying a ticket at a counter, boarding at a scheduled time, and launching from infrastructure that someone inspects and insures.

This changes liability. When a passenger steps from beach into boat and the boat tips, it is a negotiation about who pays the hospital bill. When they step from a pier and fall between the pier and the boat, it is a legal structure with clear parties. The pier operator, the boat operator, the municipality, the insurer. Someone owns that gap.

It also changes DIMAR oversight. Beach launches operate in a grey space where enforcement is complaint-driven. Pier-based operations are fixed infrastructure with published schedules. DIMAR knows where you are. Capitania knows your manifest. If your boat is not compliant, you do not leave the pier.

For operators who have been running tight, this is not expansion. It is exposure.

## How does pier infrastructure affect insurance and passenger limits?

Insurance companies price risk. A pier reduces some risks—no more passengers wading through shore break with their phones in their pockets—and makes others visible. Your passenger capacity is no longer what you tell the client. It is what the pier manifest says, and what your policy covers, and what the boat is rated for by DIMAR.

If you have been running fourteen passengers on a boat rated for ten because the extra four make the trip profitable, the pier ends that. Someone checks. Someone logs. Someone is liable when it goes wrong, and it will not be only you.

Pier infrastructure also means published schedules. If your model is we leave when we have enough people, and the pier model is departures at 9, 11, 1, and 3, you either fill those slots or you are running half-empty and losing money on fuel.

The operators who survive this are the ones who already run at capacity, with proper insurance, and with boats that pass inspection. The ones who have been getting by on flexibility and informal agreements are going to find the pier expensive.

## Who pays when shared infrastructure gets damaged?

Piers break. Cleats pull out. Fenders get stolen. Decking warps. Pilings shift. Someone has to pay for it, and the someone is usually the operator, either directly or through a maintenance fee that gets baked into the berthing cost.

If the pier is municipal, the municipality pays for capital repairs and passes the cost to you through higher fees or reduced services. If the pier is a concession, the concessionaire pays and takes it out of your margin. If the pier is a cooperative, you pay directly and spend your evenings in meetings about whether to replace the gangway now or wait until someone gets hurt.

The question no one is answering is which model Santa Marta is using. If it is concessions, expect berthing fees that price out smaller operators within two years. If it is municipal, expect delays on repairs and a slow slide toward informal side-deals to get priority access. If it is cooperative, expect the operators with the most boats to control the board.

None of these models is neutral. All of them favour someone. The time to find out who is before the pilings go in.

## What should operators be doing now?

Asking questions in writing. Not at the beach, not in a meeting, but in a document with a date on it. Where are the piers going? Who manages berthing? What are the fees? What are the insurance requirements? When is the permit process opening?

If you do not get answers, you know the process is not transparent, which tells you how it will go once the infrastructure is built. If you do get answers, you have something to hold people to when the terms change six months in.

The other thing to do is to look at your operation as it is now and decide whether it survives formalisation. If you are running legally, with proper permits, proper insurance, and boats that pass inspection, a pier is probably good for you. It is infrastructure. It makes the client experience better. It reduces your weather risk.

If you are not running that way, a pier is a countdown. You have until the concrete sets to either get compliant or get out.

The announcement says six piers. It does not say for whom.
