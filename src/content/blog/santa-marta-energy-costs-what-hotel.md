## What is the actual new energy charge hitting Santa Marta hotels?

The [new tariff announcement](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMi3AFBVV95cUxQaldXOHUzZ2VJd09UbnN4dEx2NFhlZHJRUXlfUS1iTnE0TUVjQ2tXd0I4dmFlbUt4bno0T0p4MmtGVnZoUHVLRS1oQlM5WXpHWXNVSXJPeEdkUzVtOVh1RS1ob1otdUFIbXhBM1RNU3BwUFJLcGx6YVFzSmx6SW9OVVZJVW1WMDY1MmFIWDhleERsQWlOUUJjQThPSFl3eW96Y3FONHFKcW45RDRzUzNxRWFnTUZtaWRxRjc5bVJpdkgtM0dCSVl2TmExa2VZcGZ6dEpXcDlkNFd3dXky0gH3AUFVX3lxTE42czh0bEhXOUQ4U3JKWWlZak5kU3hTd0pDdlpwdlpnMUJQNmQ2ZVFuZDM4VVV4Q1RiUFJTcDdLcDc4bWJCWXExVmE5QWpmb2NhdW1lbUVXZmJkX2EyemtuZ0xqdmkxaU5YSlE5X2dvbjFhZzFOY0VqYWt0YXh1a0F6SE9xRnZSajRNTmJwcFZraEN4OXZMN2VEQmhhSXNDTnh5Y0I0UGZYV29abzJBSmJMU0dFQnFrMjQ2cE15QVZTc2gzaUxJT05naXBWaUpweF9qNEVySU9ZWHNIS0hDaXpwZU5ZbXNKTm9DdHZ0dGxUQTdPeUVocVk?oc=5) triggered immediate warnings about rate increases and job cuts. Hotels say they cannot absorb the cost. Employees worry about layoffs. Guests will see higher room prices justified by energy bills.

But the operators who installed solar panels three years ago are not making these announcements. Neither are the ones who designed their properties around natural ventilation instead of air conditioning in every room. The tariff is the same for everyone. The reaction is not.

This is not about an energy company suddenly deciding to punish hotels. It is about which operators were already running on margins so tight that any increase—energy, labour, food—breaks the model. The tariff is just the variable that moved this month.

## How much of a hotel's cost in Santa Marta is actually energy?

For a 10-room property running air conditioning in every room, energy is typically 12 to 18 percent of operating costs during high season. For a 30-room property with a pool, restaurant and common areas, it can reach 22 percent.

But those percentages mean nothing without knowing what the total operating cost was to begin with. A hotel charging 180,000 pesos per night with 60 percent year-round occupancy has room to move. A hotel charging 120,000 pesos with 40 percent occupancy was already choosing between paying staff properly and keeping the lights on.

The operators announcing rate increases are not lying about the energy cost. They are just not mentioning that their occupancy has been below break-even for two years, or that they have been underpaying housekeeping to make payroll, or that the property was financed with a loan that assumed 75 percent occupancy that never materialised.

Energy is the cost that moved. It is not the only cost that was already unsustainable.

## Which properties were managing energy differently before this?

The ones that installed solar got laughed at in 2020. The payback period was seven years if you financed it, five if you paid cash. Everyone said it was a waste. Occupancy was the problem, not energy.

Then 2022 happened. Then 2023. The operators with solar stopped talking about payback periods and started talking about fixed costs they did not have anymore. They still pay grid connection fees. They still run generators as backup. But their exposure to tariff increases is a fraction of what it was.

The other group that adapted: properties that never installed air conditioning in half their rooms. They charge less. They get different guests. But their energy bill for a 12-room property is what a 6-room property with full AC pays, and their maintenance costs are lower because there are fewer compressors to replace.

These are not the properties announcing layoffs. They are also not the ones most guests have heard of, because they do not spend money on marketing. They spend it on infrastructure that reduces recurring costs.

## What are the actual options now besides raising rates?

Raising rates works if your occupancy can absorb it. If you are at 70 percent and your guests are not price-sensitive, add 15 percent and see what happens. If you are at 45 percent, you just made the problem worse.

Solar is still viable, but only if you can finance it without destroying your cash position for the next six months. Installation for a 10-room property runs 25 to 35 million pesos depending on consumption and roof condition. Financing exists but the interest rate is not attractive, and if your occupancy is already a problem, adding debt service is not a solution.

The option no one wants to name: reduce the service level to match what the revenue actually supports. Stop running air conditioning in common areas during the day. Stop heating the pool. Serve breakfast from 7 to 9, not 6 to 10. These are not guest experience improvements. They are cost cuts. But pretending a 40-percent-occupancy property can deliver the same service as a 75-percent-occupancy property is how you get to a place where an energy increase breaks you.

The other option: sell. If the model does not work at this cost level and you cannot change the cost structure or the revenue, the property is worth more to someone who can. That is not failure. That is math.

## Is the employment threat real or just negotiating leverage?

Both. Hotels do cut staff when costs rise. But they also threaten to cut staff every time any cost rises, because it is the threat that gets attention from government and from guests who might accept a rate increase to "save jobs."

The actual layoffs will happen in properties that were already understaffed for the occupancy they claimed to have, or overstaffed for the occupancy they actually had. A 20-room property running at 50 percent occupancy does not need the same staffing as one running at 80 percent. If it has that staffing, the energy cost is not the reason it will cut. The occupancy is.

The properties with stable staffing levels, fair pay and consistent occupancy are not making these announcements. Their labour cost as a percentage of revenue has not changed. They will absorb the energy increase the same way they absorbed the last minimum wage increase: by running a business that had room for costs to move.

The ones announcing layoffs are the ones where something else was already broken. Energy is just what moved this time.
