In September the road to the mountain is empty by four in the afternoon. Two places on the way up have their gates closed, not locked, just closed, which is how you close something you expect to open again in December. A third one is open and has nobody in it. The owner is repainting the kitchen himself because that is what you do when there is nobody to cook for.

He tells me he loses money every year in September. He says it the way you'd mention the weather.

That framing is the problem, and I used it myself for two seasons before I noticed.

## Where does the money actually go?

Low season doesn't drain you through the months with no guests. It drains you through the assumption that those months are a gap between the real ones.

The costs don't pause. Rent doesn't. Maintenance gets worse, not better, when a building sits unused in a wet climate. If you want the same staff in December, you're paying something to keep them reachable — and if you don't, you're paying in November to train people who won't be good until January.

So the fixed costs run either way. What changes is whether anything comes back from them.

## What are those months for?

This is the question that actually pays.

The things that make December work — the photos, the website that answers questions before they're asked, the relationship with the two agencies that send you the right guests, the rebuilt shower that stops being an apology — cannot be done in December. There's no time. You do them in September or you don't do them at all.

Most places don't do them at all. Then December arrives and they compete on price against everyone else who also didn't do them.

I'm not describing a discipline problem. Low season is when money is tightest, and spending it on things that pay off in three months is genuinely hard. But the alternative isn't neutral. Doing nothing has a price too, and it's charged in December, at a worse exchange rate.

## What would you actually do differently?

Pick one thing that December cannot fix.

Not five. One. The shower, or the photos, or writing down the answers to the six questions every guest asks by message before booking. Something a guest will notice in three months.

Then work out what it costs and whether the low season can carry it. If it can't, the honest answer is that it can't — and that's worth knowing in September rather than discovering in January.

The places on that road that are doing well are not the ones with the best location. They are the ones where somebody used the empty months.
